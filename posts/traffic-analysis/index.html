My Commute is a Battlefield: Analyzing Bay Area Traffic Accidents

A data-driven exploration of when and where crashes happen on the 101 & 237 corridor.

Every day, I commute from East San Jose to Palo Alto. I leave around 7-8 AM and return around 3 PM. And almost every single day, I see at least one traffic incident. Red and blue lights. Lanes blocked. Google Maps rerouting me through surface streets.

After one too many "Incident ahead, expect delays" notifications, I decided to do what any data person would do: analyze the data.

I pulled 6 years of traffic accident data (2019-2025) from TIMS (UC Berkeley's Transportation Injury Mapping System), filtered for Santa Clara County, and started digging. Here's what I found.

The "Tech Commuter" Pattern

My hypothesis: Bay Area traffic accidents would show a classic "tech worker" commute pattern—heavy peaks during rush hours on weekdays, with a completely different distribution on weekends.

The data confirms it. Look at the stark difference:

Weekdays show that distinctive "dumbbell" shape—the morning rush (7-9 AM) and the evening rush (4-7 PM). Weekends? Flat distribution. People aren't commuting; they're running errands, going to brunch, actually living their lives.

💡 Personal observation

I felt like I saw accidents "almost every day." The data backs this up: 62.5% of days have at least one accident during peak commute hours (7-10 AM, 3-7 PM). It's not just me; it's a coin flip weighted against us.

The Return-to-Office Effect

2020-2022 was the great work-from-home experiment. Then came the RTO mandates. Did returning to office change accident patterns?

The COVID era (2020-2022) shows lower rush-hour peaks. As companies mandated return to office in 2023+, we see the numbers steadily climbing back, though 2023 was still ~18% below 2019 levels. The highways are filling up again, and the accident reports are following.

When Do Crashes Happen?

The pattern is clear: Friday afternoon is the danger zone. Everyone's trying to get home early, traffic is heavy, attention is low. Wednesday and Thursday mornings are also surprisingly bad.

Are Late-Night Crashes More Severe?

Common wisdom says late-night accidents are worse—drunk driving, fatigue, empty roads encouraging speed. Let's check:

The data supports it. Night-time accidents (especially 12-6 AM) have a higher proportion of fatal and severe injuries compared to daytime rush-hour fender-benders.

COVID's Impact on Road Safety

2020 was an accidental experiment in traffic safety. Fewer cars = fewer accidents. But that didn't last:

The drop in 2020 is dramatic. By 2023-2024, traffic incidents are rebounding towards pre-pandemic levels, though still slightly below the 2019 peak. The "new normal" looks a lot like the old normal.

🚗 Data vs. Intuition

I expected the Lawrence Expressway exit or 880/Montague to top the list. But the data surprised me: US-101 at San Antonio Road is the actual #1 hotspot with over 110 accidents. It seems my "mental danger map" needs an update.

Key Takeaways

  • Rush hours are real: 7-9 AM and 4-7 PM are when most accidents happen
  • Friday afternoons are the worst: Everyone's rushing to start the weekend
  • Late-night crashes are more severe: Lower volume but higher fatality rate
  • RTO brought back the traffic: 2023+ shows pre-pandemic accident levels
  • Hotspots exist: Specific intersections and highway segments are consistently dangerous

Methodology & Data

Data source: TIMS (Transportation Injury Mapping System) from UC Berkeley SafeTREC, using SWITRS (Statewide Integrated Traffic Records System) data from CHP and local police.

Counties: Santa Clara (Palo Alto, San Jose, Milpitas)
Date range: January 2019 - September 2025
